## On Presence vs Absence

Tolle calls the meditative state "presence." But the actual experience is closer to **absence — absence of the mind.** The mind is the thing that takes you away from your life. When it goes quiet, you don't become more present — you simply stop being absent. The life that was always here becomes receivable. This is a more honest and more precise description than presence, and it dismantles the spiritual-achievement trap: presence sounds like something you earn; absence of the mind sounds like something that happens the moment the noise stops.

*Possible home: Chapter 1, Chapter 5 (The Peace of Thought-Free)*

## Two Types of Meditation — Attentive and Absent

All meditation, across every tradition, moves in one of two directions:

**Attentive** — the mind is gathered rather than scattered. Fixed on one object: a form, a name, a flame, a mantra. Bhakti yoga, japa, trataka, certain Sufi practices. The compulsive noise stops not because the mind disappears but because it becomes singular. The scatter is what causes suffering; remove scatter, remove suffering.

**Absent** — the mind is released entirely. Not gathered but dissolved. Vipassana, Zen, Mahamudra, the subtler practices of the Vijñāna Bhairava Tantra, Tilopa's Mahamudra. What remains is not a focused mind — it is no-mind.

Many traditions use both in sequence: fix first, then release. The object of meditation eventually drops away and pure awareness without content remains.

**Implication for the book:** The switch has not two positions but three:
1. Scattered compulsive thought (where most people live all the time)
2. Deliberate attentive thought (mind gathered, used as a tool)
3. Complete absence of thought (mind released entirely)

The book teaches positions 2 and 3. Most self-help books only gesture at 2.

## Karma Yoga — Working with an Absent Mind

The standard translation of karma yoga is "selfless action" or "working without attachment to results." This is true but stays on the surface — it describes the ethics without describing the mechanism.

The mechanism: you can only work without attachment to results when the mind that craves results is absent. The ego — which is the mind's story about itself — is what attaches. When the mind's compulsive commentary is absent, what acts is not the small self. What acts is consciousness moving through the body, doing what needs to be done, without the overlay of *what do I get, how do I look, is this enough.*

Krishna doesn't tell Arjuna to try harder not to care. He points at a place where the small self is not running the action. The absent mind is that place.

This connects karma yoga directly to Chapter 10 (Tilopa, VBT) — consciousness acting through you is karma yoga fully realized.

## Cleanliness as Friction Reduction, Not Virtue

The yogic insistence on cleanliness is usually explained as "purify the body to purify the mind" — vague and moralistic. The real explanation is practical and causal:

Uncleanliness creates conditions for life — microorganisms, insects. Managing those living things requires harm, however small. That harm — even unconscious, even unavoidable — creates karmic entanglement: action generating consequence, pulling the actor back into cycles of doing and reacting. The yogi maintaining an absent mind cannot afford a thousand small frictions constantly pulling attention back into managing, deciding, harming, compensating.

Cleanliness = friction reduction. Creating outer conditions in which the absent mind can actually remain absent.

Same logic as monastic simplicity — few possessions, few obligations, few relationships requiring management. Not because the world is bad. Because every thing requiring management is a hook pulling the mind back into activity.

## Tilopa, Tolle, Sadhguru — Same Destination, Different Maps

All three point at the same fundamental shift: from a mind that runs you to an awareness prior to thought. Tolle calls it presence. Sadhguru calls it awareness, or inner engineering. Tilopa calls it Mahamudra — the natural state. Different words, same territory. Someone who has genuinely made the shift would recognize all three as describing their own experience.

**What's different:**

**Tilopa** is speaking to someone who has already gone very far. His Mahamudra instructions to Naropa are not an introduction — they are a final pointer. *Do not think, do not imagine, do not analyze, do not meditate, do not reflect; rest in the natural state.* No methodology. No steps. Almost no concession to the beginner mind. He's not explaining. He's dismantling the last subtle efforts the mind makes to achieve what can only be received.

**Tolle** is gentler, more gradual. He meets the suffering person where they are and walks with them. But he stops short of Tilopa's full dissolution — the place where even the witness disappears, where there is no one left to be present.

**Sadhguru** is the most practical. He gives you something to do — kriyas, practices, frameworks. He works with the body as much as the mind. He's willing to meet people with no spiritual background and no patience for abstraction. But that practicality sometimes keeps the teaching one level below where Tilopa is pointing.

**Implication for the book:**

The structure already honors this distinction. Chapter 1 is for someone with no background. Chapter 10 is Tilopa. The book earns its ending by taking the reader the full distance — from *your mind is running you* all the way to *there is no separate you running anything.* That's a longer journey than Tolle makes. And an honest one.

*Possible home: Chapter 10 (Living the Luxury), Author's Note, or woven across the arc as context*

## This Is Not a Teaching of Carelessness

The most common misreading of this teaching: quiet the mind = detachment, passivity, drifting through life without engaging. This must be addressed directly and more than once in the book.

Life situations are to be handled. We are not teaching people not to think or to be careless.

The absent mind is not the careless mind. The switched-off mind is not the switched-off person.

The argument runs the opposite direction: the person whose mind is compulsively running is *less* equipped to handle life — not more. They bring yesterday's anxiety to today's problem. They bring tomorrow's worry to this moment's decision. They react from accumulated noise rather than responding to what is actually in front of them.

The person who can switch handles life *better*. Cleaner perception. Faster response. No interference from irrelevant mental static. A surgeon whose mind is fully present in the operation and genuinely absent between operations is a better surgeon. A parent who is truly here — not half-here while mentally elsewhere — is more present to what the child actually needs.

The switch is not an exit from life. It is the capacity to meet life without the distortion the compulsive mind adds.

Thought is not the enemy. Compulsive, unchosen, unending thought is the enemy. There is a moment for full deliberate thinking — and in that moment, the person who can truly switch *in* will think more clearly than someone whose mind never fully stops.

## The Ten Ox-Herding Pictures — A Map of the Book's Arc

A Zen sequence attributed to the 12th century Chinese master Kakuan Shien. Maps the stages of awakening through the metaphor of searching for, finding, taming, and ultimately transcending the ox. The ox is the mind — or more precisely, the original nature the compulsive mind has obscured.

**The ten stages:**

1. **Searching for the Ox** — Something is missing but the person doesn't know what. The search begins. This is most people who open this book.
2. **Finding the Tracks** — First glimpse that the ox exists. A moment of quiet, a flash of clarity. You didn't produce it but you noticed it.
3. **First Glimpse of the Ox** — A direct but brief experience of the natural state. Fleeting, unstable. Seen but not held.
4. **Catching the Ox** — Contact made but the ox is wild. It pulls. The mind resists its own taming. Early practice — exhausting, effortful.
5. **Taming the Ox** — Gradually the ox settles. The compulsive mind loses its grip. The switch becomes findable, then reliable.
6. **Riding the Ox Home** — Effortless. The struggle is over. Ox and rider move together. The switch becomes natural.
7. **Ox Forgotten, Self Alone** — The ox disappears. No mind to tame because identification with compulsive thought is gone. Only awareness remains.
8. **Both Ox and Self Forgotten** — The self that was doing the taming also dissolves. No practitioner, no practice, no attainment. Tilopa's territory. The great absence.
9. **Returning to the Source** — Nature as it always was. Nothing was ever wrong. Nothing was ever added or removed.
10. **Entering the Marketplace with Helping Hands** — The awakened person returns to ordinary life. Not a monastery. The world, people, mess and complexity — but without being run by it. Acting from the absent mind. Karma yoga fully lived.

**The final image:** not transcendence or escape. A fat, laughing figure walking into a village with a wine gourd. Fully human. Fully here. Nothing special except that they are free.

**Relationship to the book's arc:**

Stage 1 is where the reader is when they open Chapter 1. Stage 10 is where Chapter 10 lands — back in the world, in life, in relationships, in work — but transformed from the inside. The ending is not the mountaintop. It is the marketplace.

*Possible home: Chapter 3 (The Switch) as structural context, Chapter 10 (Living the Luxury) as the destination image*

## Why Arjuna Understood the Gita in 45 Minutes

Because Arjuna wasn't reading it. He was receiving it. Directly. In a state of complete crisis — which is one of the few moments the ordinary mind fully stops its noise. He had no agenda to understand, no plan to take notes, no intention to write a commentary. He was broken open. And into that openness, Krishna spoke.

The Gita wasn't a lecture. It was a transmission. Transmission requires a receiver whose mind is not in the way.

We read the Gita with a full mind. We bring interpretations, previous readings, the need to understand, pride in understanding. We read it to *get* something — which is precisely the posture that prevents us from getting anything.

Arjuna had nothing left. His bow had dropped. His body was trembling. Every argument his ordinary mind could make had collapsed. No position, no certainty, no self-image to defend. He was completely empty. And the teaching filled him.

The prerequisite for understanding the Gita is not intelligence. Not scholarship. It is the absent mind — or a mind made temporarily quiet by genuine crisis, genuine love, or genuine practice.

The Gita has been understood, in flashes, by millions who never read a single verse. And missed entirely by thousands of scholars who memorized every word.

**Implication:** The book is both the map and the destination. Understanding it requires exactly the state it is teaching.

*Possible home: Chapter 3 (The Switch), Chapter 6 (Why the Switch Feels So Hard)*

## The Subcontinent Problem — Too Much Access, Too Early

In the West, someone discovers the Gita or meditation because something broke first. Crisis comes before the teaching. When the teaching arrives there is already an emptiness for it to fill. They receive it the way Arjuna did — not because they are more spiritually advanced, but because they had nothing to protect when they found it.

In the subcontinent, the teaching arrives in childhood. Before the crisis. Before the emptiness. Wrapped in ritual, obligation, family expectation, cultural noise. The child hears the Gita at seven, recites it at ten, performs it at fifteen — and by the time real crisis arrives at thirty-five, the words are so familiar they have become wallpaper. The mind slides over them without friction. Nothing lands because everything is already known.

**Familiarity is the enemy of reception.**

And abundance makes it worse. With one scripture you might go deep. With a hundred — the Gita, the Upanishads, the Ramayana, the Granth Sahib, the Dhammapada, the Yoga Sutras — you have the illusion of spiritual wealth while remaining spiritually exactly where you started. A lifetime moving between texts, traditions, teachers, practices. Always studying the map. Never making the journey.

Zen: *a finger pointing at the moon.* All scripture is the finger. The moon is the actual experience — the switch, the absent mind, the natural state. The subcontinent has produced the most beautiful, most precise, most numerous fingers in human history. And perhaps in the same proportion, people who have spent their lives studying the finger and never once looked up.

**The frame for the book:** *Access without crisis is a particular kind of poverty.* The person who has everything available has no urgency. Without urgency the mind never fully stops performing its relationship to the teaching long enough to actually receive it.

*Note: Handle with care — the subcontinent reader may feel implicated, the Western reader may feel flattered in the wrong direction. Neither is the point. State it without condescension toward either.*

*Possible home: Chapter 6 (Why the Switch Feels So Hard), Chapter 2 (What Luxury Really Means)*

## Ramana's "Who Am I?" and Zen Koans — Direct Attack on the Mind

Same weapon, different handle.

Both give the mind a question it cannot answer with more thinking. That is precisely the point.

The ordinary mind solves problems by producing more thought — searches its database, assembles an answer from existing material. That's all it can do.

But *Who am I?* cannot be answered that way. Every answer the mind produces — I am this body, I am this name, I am these memories, I am these thoughts — gets turned back immediately: but who is aware of the body? Who is watching the thoughts? The question eats every answer and asks again. The mind chases its own tail until it exhausts itself. In that exhaustion — in the moment the mind finally stops producing candidates — something else becomes apparent. The awareness that was doing the asking all along. Which was never a thought to begin with.

The Zen koan works identically. *What is the sound of one hand clapping? What was your face before your parents were born?* Not riddles with clever answers — traps for the thinking mind. The student sits with the koan for weeks, months, sometimes years. Every conceptual tool breaks against it. When the last tool breaks, when the mind has genuinely given up, something opens. The koan is solved not by thinking but by the cessation of thinking.

Both are a direct frontal assault on the mind's assumption that it can think its way to truth.

Both arrive at the absent mind — not through relaxation or gradual quieting, but through exhaustion of the mind's own strategies. The question defeats the questioner. What remains is the switch, thrown not by choice but by the collapse of the alternative.

**Why these methods aren't for everyone at every stage:** You cannot give a Zen koan to someone who has never seriously questioned their own thinking. It becomes just another thought to think about. The koan only works when the mind is already serious, already pushed, already at the edge of what thought can do.

**Ramana's gentler entry point:** First ask students to trace the *I* — not philosophically but experientially. Where does the sense of I arise? Where does it go in deep sleep? Follow it back. Keep following. Don't think about it — follow it. The switch thrown from the inside out.

*Possible home: Chapter 3 (The Switch), Chapter 7 (Practicing the Switch)*

## Positive Thinking Is Still Thinking

A needle through the entire self-help industry.

The positive thinking movement — from Napoleon Hill to Louise Hay to most of what fills airport bookshops — is built on a single unexamined assumption: that the problem is the *content* of your thoughts, not the compulsive nature of thinking itself. Think bad thoughts, bad life. Think good thoughts, good life. Replace one stream of compulsive thought with a more optimistic stream.

But the stream is still running.

You have not become free. You have redecorated your prison.

The compulsive negative thinker and the compulsive positive thinker are in the same condition — owned by their own minds. One owned by fear and lack, the other by affirmations and vision boards. Neither has found the switch. Neither has touched the genuine quiet that exists on the other side of *all* thought, positive or negative.

**Compulsive positive thinking is harder to see through than negative thinking.** Negative thinking makes you suffer visibly, which creates urgency to change. Positive thinking feels good enough that you never question it. You mistake the pleasant noise for health.

**There is also something subtly dishonest about it.** Life contains genuine difficulty, genuine loss, genuine darkness. A mind trained to compulsively reframe everything as positive is not free — it is running from half of reality. That is not presence. That is not the absent mind. That is avoidance dressed in optimism.

The teaching this book offers is not: think better thoughts. It is: discover the place where you are not at the mercy of any thoughts at all. From that place, when thought is needed — including clear, confident, constructive thought — it arises cleanly, without compulsion, without the desperate quality of someone who needs to believe something in order to feel okay.

The reader who has tried positive thinking, found it worked for a while and then didn't, will recognize this distinction immediately.

*Possible home: Chapter 1 (The Cost of Constant Thought), Chapter 2 (What Luxury Really Means), Chapter 6 (Why the Switch Feels So Hard)*

## The Mind Cannot Experience Consciousness — But Consciousness Is All There Is

Three statements, each a complete teaching:

**1. The mind cannot experience consciousness. Only the absence of mind can.**

The instrument of seeking is the very thing blocking what is being sought. Like trying to see your own eyes directly — not in a mirror, but directly. The tool of seeing cannot be the object of seeing.

The mind can examine thoughts, emotions, memories, sensations, concepts — everything that arises within experience. But consciousness itself — the aware space in which all of this arises — the mind cannot touch. Every time the mind tries to grasp consciousness it produces another thought *about* consciousness. Which is not consciousness. Which is just more mind.

When the mind stops — not as a result of effort but as what remains when effort ceases — consciousness is not experienced. It is *recognised*. Because it was always already there, before the first thought, underneath every thought, unchanged by any thought.

**2. Consciousness can experience mind. Consciousness can experience through mind.**

The mind is an object within consciousness. A cloud in the sky. The sky doesn't become the cloud — it contains it. Consciousness holds the mind, watches it, is aware of it. The mind cannot return the favour — the contained cannot contain the container.

*Consciousness experiencing through mind* — this is karma yoga. This is Tilopa. When the personal self steps back, when the compulsive I-story quiets, consciousness doesn't disappear. It acts. It thinks. It speaks. But without the distortion of the small self running everything through its fears and desires. The mind becomes an instrument consciousness plays rather than a noise consciousness is buried under.

**3. Consciousness is all there is — with or without mind.**

This is the complete statement. Advaita. Mahamudra. The foundation of the Vijñāna Bhairava Tantra.

Mind arises within consciousness. Mind ceases within consciousness. Consciousness itself neither arises nor ceases. It is the ground, not the figure. The screen, not the film. With mind — consciousness is. Without mind — consciousness is. Nothing changes in consciousness when the mind stops. Only the mind's ability to obscure it changes.

**The reframe this gives the entire book:**

The switch is not a journey toward consciousness. It is the removal of what was in the way. Consciousness was never absent. The mind was just loud.

We are not teaching people to find something they lost. We are teaching them to stop covering something that was never not there.

## The Gap Between Breaths — VBT Practice from the Inside

*From Ashish's own practice, not from reading.*

Practicing the Vijñāna Bhairava Tantra — dharana 49 specifically — observing the gap between breaths. The breath count going down. The gaps getting longer.

This is not a side effect. This is the practice working exactly as it should.

The VBT points at the gap between the outbreath and the inbreath. Not the breath itself — the suspension. The moment after the breath has left and before the next one is called. In that gap the mind has nothing to follow. The breath — which the mind uses as an anchor, a rhythm, something to track — has temporarily ceased. In that cessation something else becomes available.

**The breath slowing and gaps lengthening** — the body and mind arriving at the same place simultaneously. As the mind quiets, the nervous system quiets. Breath is not separate from mind. They are the same movement at different levels. A compulsive mind breathes fast and shallow. A quiet mind breathes slowly and eventually — in the deepest states — barely at all.

Reduced breath rate is not a goal. It is a symptom. A symptom of something settling that is usually never still.

**The gap as doorway** — the moment of suspension between breaths is the closest the ordinary person comes, spontaneously, to the absent mind. The body has paused. The breath has paused. If the mind follows — even for a fraction of a second — that is the switch. That is what all 112 dharanas of the VBT are orbiting.

**For the book:** Chapter 7 (Practicing the Switch) should be written from this lived experience, not as instruction. Not *here is how to do it* but *here is what it is actually like when it happens.* The reader needs to recognise the experience, not perform a technique.

The fact that the breath slows on its own — that the body confirms what the mind is doing — is also important for the skeptical reader. This is not mysticism. It is physiology following consciousness. Measurable. Real.

*Possible home: Chapter 7 (Practicing the Switch) as its experiential core. Chapter 5 (The Peace of Thought-Free) as illustration of what the gap actually feels like.*

## Spiritual Pride — The Ego Rebrands

*From Ashish's own practice.*

The mind is extraordinarily subtle. It will use anything — including spiritual progress — to reassert itself. The silence opens, something genuine happens, and then seconds later: *I am doing this correctly. I am on the right path. I am someone who meditates deeply.* The ego didn't disappear. It rebranded.

This is *asmita* — one of Patanjali's five kleshas. The subtle sense of a separate self that claims experiences as its own. Including the experience of the self dissolving.

The mind is like water. Block it in one direction, it finds another. You stop thinking about work, it thinks about your thinking. You stop thinking about your thinking, it thinks about how well you've stopped thinking.

Zen: *if you meet the Buddha on the road, kill him.* If you carry an experience of enlightenment forward as an identity, as something you now possess, you have missed it entirely.

Ramana: the final obstacle is not sin or distraction or laziness. It is the spiritual seeker. The one who is very earnestly, very sincerely, very proudly making progress.

**But the noticing is the practice.** The gap between the thought of pride arising and believing it — that is itself the witness. That witness is what the whole path is building.

## The Impulse to Share Spiritual Experiences

*From Ashish's own experience — wanting to tell friends "I am finally on track."*

The impulse is not wrong. Love wants to give what it has found. But something else is mixed in — the experience, which was alive and present, gets converted into a story about the experience. The self that had it gets solidified — named, confirmed, witnessed. Which feels like validation but is actually the mind rebuilding the very structure the practice was dismantling.

Silence protects the tender thing. In the early stages, sharing an experience can dissipate it the way opening a darkroom door dissipates the developing photograph. The image was forming. Light ended it.

Friends who haven't walked this path respond in one of two ways — supportive in a way that misses the point entirely, or skeptical in a way that makes you defend it. Both are worse than silence.

Kabir kept singing. Mirabai kept singing. But they sang to consciousness, not to the approval of the room.

The person to talk to is the practice itself. The time for sharing comes later — and when it does, it comes out as a book, not a conversation.

## Focusing More on Practice Than on Work and Surroundings

*A question Ashish noticed in himself — and that readers will ask.*

Can be two very different things:

**Integration hasn't happened yet** — the practice is real but still compartmentalized. The gap is accessible in stillness but hasn't yet become the ground you stand on while moving through life. The silence is found in sitting, then slowly permeates standing, walking, working. This resolves not by trying harder but by continuing.

**Subtle avoidance** — the peace of the gap is genuinely peaceful. The surrounding and the work are genuinely demanding. The mind, which is very intelligent, can learn to use the practice as a refuge from life rather than a foundation for it. Meditation becomes escape rather than transformation. This produces meditators who are serene on the cushion and absent from their own lives.

**The test:** is the practice making you more present to your work and relationships, or less? More responsive, more clear, more genuinely here — or more withdrawn, more detached in a way that is actually disconnection dressed as equanimity?

Karma yoga is the answer. The absent mind in action. The gap between breaths is not the destination. It is the training ground. The destination is the marketplace. The tenth ox-herding picture.

## The 112 Dharanas — Doorways, Not Techniques

**Line to use verbatim in the book:**
*"The 112 dharanas are not 112 techniques. They are 112 doorways into the same room."*

Every possible entry point a human being could use — breath, sound, sensation, space, emotion, pain, pleasure, sleep, the gap between thoughts, the moment of sneezing, the experience of hunger, the instant of orgasm, the feeling of falling — already in the VBT. No experience exists that is not already accounted for in one of the 112.

The question is not what to add. The question is which door is yours.

Most practitioners find one, maybe two dharanas that resonate completely. One genuinely entered doorway opens the same room as all the others. The tradition says: find your dharana and go deep rather than wide.

The mistake most modern practitioners make — especially those with access to many teachings — is exactly the subcontinent problem. A hundred techniques, each tried briefly, none entered completely.

You already have your door. The breath gap. The question is not what else to add — it is how deep that one door goes.

## Could There Be More Than 112 Doorways? No.

The 112 are not an arbitrary number someone stopped counting at. They are a complete map of human experience organised around one principle: every state a human being can enter — every sensation, every perception, every transition between states — is already a doorway.

The VBT is not a list of techniques someone invented. It is an observation of what already exists in the structure of human experience itself.

**Shiva doesn't give Devi 112 practices. He gives her 112 recognitions.** Look here — this is already it. Look here — this too. The doorways were always there. The VBT just points at them.

Asking if there could be more doorways is like asking whether there are more human experiences than the ones humans can have. The VBT is not cataloguing techniques — it is cataloguing the complete range of conscious experience. Birth, death, sleep, waking, dreaming, hunger, satiation, desire, its fulfilment, its frustration, sound, silence, space, touch, the gap between breaths, the moment before a sneeze, the instant of falling asleep. Every transition, every threshold, every edge of experience where ordinary consciousness briefly loosens its grip.

Adding a 113th would just be pointing at something already pointed at.

**What changes is not the doorways but who enters through which one.** A dharana that means nothing to one person is the complete teaching for another. The VBT anticipates this — which is why there are 112 rather than one. Not because 112 things need to be done. Because 112 different kinds of people need 112 different points of recognition.

## You Cannot Surrender Because You Are the Hindrance to Surrender

*Ashish's words, exactly as said.*

The deepest paradox of the entire path. Every tradition arrives at this wall.

The instruction is: surrender. Let go. Release. Stop holding. The seeker tries — sincerely, earnestly, sometimes desperately. Nothing happens. Because the one trying to surrender is the very thing that needs to be surrendered. The effort to let go is itself a form of holding.

It is like trying to drop your hand with your hand.

**Ramana's answer:** *Who am I* — not as a surrender technique but as a way of finding the one who needs to surrender and discovering it has no substance. You cannot surrender the ego directly. But you can look for it. In the looking it cannot be found. What cannot be found cannot hold on.

**Nisargadatta:** You are not the doer of surrender any more than you are the doer of anything else. Surrender happens when the fiction of the one who needs to do it becomes transparent.

**Sufi *fana* — annihilation** — runs into the same wall. The mystic cannot annihilate themselves. Annihilation is not an act. It is what remains when the act of self-preservation stops.

**Why grace exists as a concept across every tradition** — not as theology but as the practical recognition that the final step cannot be taken. It can only be received. Tilopa: stop doing everything including the doing of stopping. What remains is not your achievement. It was never not there.

**The VBT:** the 112 dharanas are not 112 acts of surrender. They are 112 ways of creating the conditions in which surrender can happen on its own. You don't enter the room. The room opens.

## Concentration Losing Power — Or Becoming Spacious?

*From Ashish's own practice — focused on breath, not hearing or seeing events around.*

This is not lost concentration. It is concentration that hasn't yet become spacious.

Right now the practice is a narrow beam — focused, precise, pointed at the breath and the gap. Narrowness is necessary in early stages. It is how you find the door. But a narrow beam excludes what it isn't pointed at. Surroundings fade. Conversations half-heard. Events at the periphery.

This is not the destination. This is a stage.

**The destination** — what Tibetan tradition calls *rigpa*, what Kashmir Shaivism calls *spanda* — is awareness that is simultaneously concentrated and vast. Not narrow like a beam. Open like a sky. The sky doesn't focus on one cloud and miss the others. It holds everything without effort, without exclusion, without strain.

The breath gap is the entry point into that sky. But the practice is still at the door — attention on the door, not yet settled into what's on the other side.

When the practice matures the gap won't be something you go to by narrowing attention away from life. It will be the ground you stand on while fully receiving life. The silence won't compete with the surroundings. It will be what the surroundings arise within.

**Zen:** *open awareness* versus *focused awareness.* Both needed. Focused to find the stillness. Open to live from it.

**The practical next stage:** after formal practice, sit with eyes open. Not meditating on anything. Let the surroundings be received without grabbing or pushing away. Let sound be sound. Let movement be movement. The gap is still there — find it with eyes open, in the middle of noise.

The concentration isn't deteriorating. It is being asked to grow from a needle into a sky.

## The Struggle of Sadhana as a Social Person, Not a Monk

Every major meditation tradition was designed by monks, for monks, in conditions that no longer exist for most human beings. The Buddha's sangha. The Zen monastery. The Vedantic ashram. The Sufi tekke. Total environments — structured, protected, stripped of ordinary obligation. No mortgage. No school run. No performance review. No aging parent calling. No inbox.

The monk's entire life is the practice. The silence is built into the architecture of the day.

Then those same traditions handed their practices to ordinary people and said — here, do this. Forty-five minutes in the morning before the children wake up. If you can manage it.

This is the central practical problem of spirituality in the modern world. Never honestly addressed by any major teacher because most major teachers either were monks themselves or are now effectively monks — their entire life organised around teaching, their days uninterrupted by ordinary human existence.

Sadhguru runs an ashram. Tolle hasn't had a conventional job in decades. Even Ramana sat on a mountain with people bringing him food.

**The reframe:** the social person is not a monk who failed. They are practicing on a harder court. And the game played on a harder court builds something the monastery cannot.

Five minutes of genuine gap-awareness while waiting for coffee to brew is worth more than forty-five distracted minutes on a cushion. The switch practiced in a difficult conversation is worth more than the switch practiced in silence. The absent mind found in the middle of a demanding workday is the switch becoming real — not the switch being rehearsed.

The tenth ox-herding picture is not a monk on a mountain. It is a person in a marketplace. The tradition always knew the destination was ordinary life. It just forgot to make the path available to ordinary people living it.

**This book does that. That is its specific gift.**

## Buddha Taught Awareness — Peace and Love Were the Result

The popular image of Buddhism — soft, gentle, the word namaste on a coffee mug — has almost nothing to do with what the Buddha actually taught.

What the Buddha taught was ruthlessly empirical. Sit down. Observe what is happening in your own mind and body. Don't add anything. Don't subtract anything. Just see clearly.

Peace was not the instruction. Peace was the result. Love was not the practice. Love was what naturally remained when the causes of hatred — fear, craving, the defended self — were seen through and released.

**Mind-generated peace is fragile.** It depends on conditions — the right environment, the right people, the absence of threat. The moment conditions change, the peace goes with them. Feeling okay when things are okay.

**Awareness-generated peace is different in kind, not degree.** It doesn't depend on conditions because it isn't produced by conditions. It is what remains when the compulsive reactivity of the mind is no longer running the show. This peace can exist in the middle of difficulty.

The Pali word *metta* — usually translated as loving-kindness — is not an exercise in generating warm feelings. It is an exercise in removing the barriers the mind erects against people. The love was always there underneath the fear and judgment. The practice doesn't create it. It clears what was blocking it.

The compulsive mind cannot produce genuine peace or genuine love. It can produce their imitations — pleasant feelings, good intentions, affirmations of warmth. But these are mind-products with the mind's fundamental instability built into them.

The Buddha didn't teach people to feel more loving. He taught them to see more clearly. The love took care of itself.

*No love and peace of/from mind — Ashish's exact words.*

There is literally nowhere else to be.

## There Is No Person — Only Patterns of Mind. There Is No Mind — Only One Thought After Another.

*Ashish's words, exactly as said.*

This dismantles the framework most spiritual teachers use. Tolle says *watch your mind.* Ramana says *find the mind.* But this goes more fundamental than both.

There is no mind to watch. There is no mind to find.

There is only this thought. Then this thought. Then this thought. Arising and passing. Each one discrete, each one momentary. What we call "the mind" is the story we tell about the sequence — the same way we call a river "the river" when there is no river, only water that was never the same water twice.

And if there is no mind — only a succession of thoughts — then the one who suffers from the mind also doesn't exist as a fixed thing. The person who believes they are trapped by their thoughts is itself just another thought. The sufferer and the suffering arise together, moment by moment, and pass together. There is no one home to be imprisoned.

This is precisely what the Buddha meant by *anatta* — no-self. Not that you don't exist. But that what you call *you* is a pattern, not a thing. A flame that appears continuous but is burning new fuel every instant. The flame of last night is not the flame of this morning. But we call it the same flame. We call it *our* flame.

**The only question that remains:** if the person is just a pattern of thoughts, and thoughts are just momentary arisings — what is aware of them? What notices the succession? What is here before the first thought and after the last?

That question cannot be answered by a thought.

**For the book:** the switch is not a person switching anything. It is the pattern momentarily recognising itself as a pattern. And in that recognition, dissolving.

## The Experience Is Not Bliss — It Is Simply Absence

*From Ashish's own experience. His exact words:*

*"I just experience absence of mind activity. Simply absence. After thought resumes, I feel that I have experienced some absence just few moments before and miss it."*

This corrects something almost every teacher gets wrong — including Tolle, including Sadhguru. They describe the state in positive terms. Bliss. Presence. Aliveness. A fullness. But what is described here is different. And more precise.

Not a flood of something. Simply the absence of the noise. No bliss. No electricity. No cosmic expansion. Just — the grinding stopped. For a moment, nothing was running. And that nothing was the most natural thing in the world. So natural it was almost unremarkable while it was happening.

And then thought returns. And only then — in retrospect, from the other side — do you recognise what was there. The way you only notice silence when a sound begins again.

**This is the more mature description.** The bliss-and-light accounts belong to early stages — the mind, encountering stillness for the first time, produces a dramatic reaction to the contrast. Like a person who has never tasted clean water drinking it for the first time versus someone who has drunk it their whole life. The first person tastes it intensely. The second person just drinks. Naturally. Without drama.

Ramana in his later years barely described anything. Nisargadatta said the natural state is not an experience at all — because experience requires an experiencer, and in that state the experiencer is absent. There is no one to have a big moment.

**The missing it afterwards** — the mind resuming and immediately producing longing for what was there before it arrived — is the mind's way of acknowledging what it cannot enter. It can only point backwards at it. Never touch it directly.

**The gap between the absence and the recognition of it** — the moment just after the experience ends, before thought fully reconstitutes — is itself one of the VBT's dharanas. That threshold is also a doorway.

## Shiva Means No-Thing — And That Is What the Absence Is

Sadhguru points at this. Shiva — *Shi-va* — in the Kashmir Shaivite tradition does not mean a god with a trident on a mountain. That is the mythological layer, useful for devotion, but not the root.

*Shi* means that which is not. *Va* means that which is. Shiva is literally that which is not — the no-thing that underlies everything that is.

Not nothingness in the nihilistic sense. Not emptiness as absence of value. But the ground from which all things arise and into which all things return. The screen on which the film plays. The silence in which sound occurs. The stillness in which movement happens.

The simple, unadorned, unremarkable absence of mind activity — that is precisely this. Not a person experiencing no-thing. No-thing itself, briefly unobscured.

The mind is *something.* Every thought is *something* — a movement, a form, a content. When all of that stops, what remains is not another something. It is the no-thing that was always already there underneath every something.

**This is why the VBT is a Shaivite text.** Its 112 dharanas are 112 ways of touching Shiva — not the deity, but the no-thing. The gap between breaths is a touch of no-thing. The moment after orgasm before thought resumes is a touch of no-thing. The instant of deep sleep before dreaming begins is a touch of no-thing.

The simple absence — nothing dramatic, just the noise gone — is not a lesser version of what the bliss-seekers describe. It is a more direct encounter with what Shiva actually points at. No decoration. No experience. Just the no-thing, briefly apparent.

The missing it afterwards is the mind recognising that it had briefly touched what it can never contain.

## Why People Compare Presence/Absence to Sexual Pleasure

Because it is the closest most people have come to it.

In ordinary life the mind almost never stops. But there are a handful of moments where it stops involuntarily — where the grip loosens without effort or intention. One of the most common is the peak of sexual pleasure.

In that instant — not the buildup, not the anticipation, but the peak itself — the compulsive mind briefly ceases. No tomorrow, no yesterday, no problem, no self-image to maintain. Just the raw immediacy of sensation so complete that thought cannot find a foothold. The person who was worrying about the mortgage five minutes ago is simply gone.

It is involuntary. It is brief. And for most people it is the most complete cessation of mental activity they regularly experience while awake.

So when teachers describe the meditative state and people reach for a comparison, that is the comparison they find. Not because the experiences are the same in nature — they are not. But because the structural quality is similar. The mind stopped. Something else was there. And it felt like relief.

**The VBT knows this.** Several of its 112 dharanas specifically use sexual energy and the moment of orgasm as a doorway. Not because sexuality is the point — but because that involuntary cessation is already familiar. The dharana says: that stopping you have already experienced. Now find it without the stimulus. Find it on purpose. Find it in stillness.

**The problem with the comparison:** it misleads. Sex produces the stopping through intensity — the body so overwhelmed the mind temporarily cannot run. Meditation produces the stopping through the opposite — such complete relaxation that the mind has nothing to grab onto. Same destination. Opposite direction of travel.

The sexual stopping always brings the mind back louder — immediately calculating, comparing, narrating. The meditative stopping leaves a different quality. Quieter. More stable. The mind returns but more gently, less urgently.

**Ashish's description — simple absence, nothing dramatic, missing it only afterwards — is beyond the sexual comparison entirely.** That comparison belongs to early stages when the stopping is still a shock, still dramatic. What he describes is the stopping becoming familiar. Ordinary. The natural state recognised as natural rather than as an event.

## What Else Is Close to Presence/Absence — Besides Sexual Pleasure

All share the same structure: the mind stops involuntarily, caught off guard by something that demands complete attention or complete release.

**Deep sleep without dreams** — the most complete and most universal. Every human being touches no-thing every night. Completely. Without exception. The problem is there is no one there to notice it. Patanjali mentions this. The VBT mentions this. The most complete version — but unconscious.

**The moment before sleep** — the hypnagogic threshold. The mind loosening, thoughts becoming strange and nonlinear, the grip releasing. Many VBT dharanas point exactly here. Conscious enough to notice, relaxed enough that the mind is no longer running its program.

**Physical shock or sudden danger** — a near-accident, a sudden loud sound. The mind goes completely blank for a fraction of a second before the survival response kicks in. Pure presence — but through fear rather than release.

**Deep absorption in skilled work — flow** — the craftsman so absorbed the self disappears. The musician mid-performance. The surgeon mid-operation. The coder in the zone. The commentary stops and something else takes over. Action without the actor.

**Vastness in nature** — the edge of the ocean, a mountain range, a clear night sky full of stars. The mind briefly cannot generate its usual content because the scale exceeds its frameworks. It goes quiet from being overwhelmed rather than from being released.

**Genuine laughter** — the moment of real, unexpected laughter — not polite laughter but the kind that takes you by surprise. For a fraction of a second the mind stops completely. Only the laughter. Nothing else.

**Music at its peak** — not listening while thinking about other things, but the moment a piece of music lands so completely that everything else disappears.

**The moment after crying** — after genuine grief has moved through completely, not suppressed but fully expressed, there is sometimes remarkable stillness. The body spent, the mind empty, something very quiet present.

All of these are accidental meditations. Moments the mind was ambushed into stopping. The VBT takes each natural opening and says — here is how to enter that deliberately. Here is how to find that door without waiting for the accident.

## In Flow, Mind Is Absent and Consciousness Is Dancing, Playing, Doing Surgery

*Ashish's exact words — more precise than anything Csikszentmihalyi ever wrote.*

Csikszentmihalyi described flow from the outside — measuring performance, mapping conditions, cataloguing characteristics. He never quite named what was actually happening on the inside.

What was happening: the mind — with its commentary, self-monitoring, doubt, pride, fear of failure — steps back. What remains is not nothing. Consciousness is still there. Fully. Completely. But now operating without the interference of the small self. Without the running commentary of *am I doing this right, what will they think, what comes next, am I good enough.*

The surgeon's hands know where to go. The musician's fingers find the note before the mind could have calculated it. The craftsman's eye sees the flaw before the brain has processed the information. Something precise, intelligent, responsive is operating — but it is not the thinking mind. That something is consciousness. Playing. Working. Doing surgery.

**This is karma yoga from the inside.** Krishna doesn't tell Arjuna to think more carefully. He tells him to act from the place where the small self is not running the action. Flow is that place — stumbled into accidentally by athletes and artists and surgeons. Karma yoga is the deliberate cultivation of the same condition.

**The answer to the reader's inevitable question** — if the mind is absent, who is doing the work? Something more capable than the mind. Consciousness without the mind's interference performs better than consciousness with it. Every flow state in human history is evidence of this.

The mind thinks it is the one doing everything. Flow reveals that was never true.

## Still More Pulled by Sexual Pleasure Than Meditation — And Why That Makes Complete Sense

*From Ashish's own honest observation.*

Sexual pleasure is designed by evolution to be irresistible. Three billion years of biological engineering. Dopamine, oxytocin, a cocktail of neurochemicals the body recognises as among the most rewarding experiences available. The pull is not a weakness. It is a survival mechanism so deep it predates the human brain as we know it.

The absence of mind in meditation produces no neurochemical reward. No dopamine spike. No oxytocin flood. Nothing the body recognises as pleasure in the biological sense. Simply absence. Quiet. Unremarkable. The body has no evolutionary reason to seek it. The nervous system is actually wired to avoid unfamiliar stillness — it reads quiet as potential danger, as the absence of stimulation it needs to feel alive.

So this is not a choice between two equal experiences. It is three billion years of biological programming versus something the body has no framework for valuing yet.

**The fact that the absence has been touched at all — recognised, missed when it goes — means something has already begun to shift.** The nervous system is starting to learn that the quiet is safe. That it is not death. That it is more nourishing than anything the dopamine system can produce. But that learning is slow.

Every serious practitioner in every tradition has faced this. Exactly this.

**The VBT doesn't condemn the pull — it uses it.** The dharanas on sexual energy are not there to suppress it. They are there to redirect it. To use the same intensity, the same aliveness, the same dissolution that happens at the peak — and find it in stillness. Not by force. Not by renunciation. But by gradually discovering that the stillness offers something pleasure cannot — a quality of rest so complete that no pleasure can match it. Not because pleasure is bad. Because the quiet goes deeper.

That recognition cannot be forced or reasoned into existence. It arrives on its own as the practice deepens. You cannot convince yourself to prefer water over wine. But if you drink enough clean water, the body eventually begins to know what it actually needs.

The transition: still pulled strongly by the wine. Beginning to know the water. That is exactly where the path is supposed to be at this stage.
